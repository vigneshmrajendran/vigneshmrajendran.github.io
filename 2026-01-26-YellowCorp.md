# Yellow Corporation Bankruptcy Analysis

## The Surface Story: Everything Looked Fine

Yellow Corporation's 2022 financial ratios told a turnaround story. Revenue grew 2.4% to $5.2 billion. Operating cash flow turned positive at $121 million. Debt service coverage ratio climbed to 1.6x—above the minimum 1.25x covenant threshold. The Altman Z-Score sat at 2.57, suggesting stability. Gross margins held steady at 11.66%, typical for trucking operations.

Eight months later, Yellow filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

## What the Ratios Missed

The problem wasn't operations. Yellow's core business functioned. Drivers hauled freight. Customers paid invoices. The company generated positive operating cash flow every year from 2018 through 2022 except a brief dip in 2019.

The problem was what happened after operations—in the financing and capital expenditure decisions that don't show up in operating metrics.

## The Capital Expenditure Trap

Yellow's historical capital expenditure ran $85-90 million annually for routine fleet maintenance and replacement. In 2020, the U.S. government provided a $700 million COVID relief loan. Management saw an opportunity: buy trucks now while credit markets were closed to competitors, satisfy union demands for newer equipment, and grab market share.

In 2021, Yellow spent $494 million on revenue equipment—4.5 times their historical average.

Here's what that did to cash flow:

2021 operating cash flow: +$10 million
2021 capital expenditure: -$484 million
2021 free cash flow: -$474 million

The company burned through $474 million in a single year despite generating positive operating cash. Over the 2019-2022 period, cumulative free cash flow totaled negative $570 million.

## The Debt Maturity Wall

The $700 million government loan came with standard terms: five-year amortization. Yellow also carried existing debt from earlier restructurings. By 2022, the debt maturity schedule showed $1.3 billion coming due in 2024.

This created an impossible situation. Yellow needed to refinance $1.3 billion while carrying:
- Total debt of $1.57 billion
- EBITDA of only $341 million
- Debt-to-EBITDA ratio of 7.4x

No commercial lender refinances a trucking company at 7.4x leverage, regardless of what the debt service coverage ratio shows.

## When Coverage Ratios Lie

Yellow's 1.6x debt service coverage ratio in 2022 looked adequate. The ratio measures operating cash flow against required debt payments. Yellow could cover current interest and principal obligations.

But debt service coverage assumes debt rolls over. It measures whether you can pay this year's bill. It doesn't ask whether anyone will lend you money next year.

With $1.3 billion maturing in 2024 and leverage at 7.4x, the coverage ratio became meaningless. Yellow didn't need to cover debt service—they needed to refinance the entire principal. That required lender confidence Yellow couldn't provide.

## The Freight Recession Timing

Yellow's fleet expansion bet on continued tight capacity and strong pricing from the post-COVID freight boom. Instead, 2022-2023 brought a freight recession. Shipping volumes dropped. Pricing power evaporated. Companies brought transportation in-house rather than using contract carriers.

The same market conditions that made the 2021 equipment purchase seem strategic made the 2024 refinancing impossible. Lenders saw:
- Deteriorating freight fundamentals
- Rising interest rates increasing refinancing costs
- A company that just burned $570 million on fleet expansion
- Regulatory uncertainty from California's AB5 employment law

Every refinancing conversation ended the same way: no.

## What Midcap Companies Miss

Most small and midcap companies track operating cash flow and stop there. Yellow's operating cash flow was positive. The monthly dashboard showed green.

But free cash flow—operating cash flow minus capital expenditures—told the real story. Yellow looked healthy on operating metrics while bleeding cash on the financing side.

The second missed metric: debt maturity schedule. Yellow's coverage ratios suggested they could service debt indefinitely. The maturity schedule showed they had eighteen months to find $1.3 billion or file bankruptcy.

## The Two Numbers That Mattered

By 2023, only two numbers determined Yellow's survival:

Free cash flow: How much cash remained after required equipment spending?
Answer: Negative $570 million cumulative from 2019-2022.

Debt maturity: How much needed refinancing and when?
Answer: $1.3 billion due in 2024.

Every other ratio—debt service coverage, interest coverage, Altman Z-Score, operating cash flow—measured secondary factors. Yellow didn't fail operations. They failed financing.

## What Killed Yellow

The bankruptcy resulted from three connected decisions:

First, management used cheap government debt to fund a massive equipment purchase during a freight boom. Capital expenditures jumped from $90 million annually to $494 million in 2021.

Second, this spending created negative free cash flow of $474 million in 2021 despite positive operating cash flow. Yellow burned through their government loan buying trucks instead of building cash reserves.

Third, the debt came with a five-year amortization schedule, creating a $1.3 billion maturity wall in 2024. When freight markets turned and interest rates rose, no lender would refinance a 7.4x leveraged trucking company.

Yellow's 2021 capital expenditure decision—made during loose credit and strong freight demand—locked in bankruptcy math that played out over the next two years. By the time the debt came due in 2024, market conditions had shifted completely. The refinancing that seemed certain in 2021 became impossible in 2023.

## The Lesson

Yellow Corporation proves that positive operating cash flow and adequate coverage ratios don't guarantee survival. Two factors matter more:

Free cash flow after required capital expenditures—not just operating cash flow.

Debt maturity timing and refinancing risk—not just current debt service coverage.

A company can generate positive operating cash, meet all debt covenants, maintain stable margins, and still fail if they burn cash on capital expenditures while carrying refinancing risk they can't manage.

Yellow's management made an opportunistic bet on fleet expansion using cheap government debt. The bet assumed continued strong freight markets and easy refinancing. When both assumptions failed, the $1.3 billion maturity wall became a bankruptcy trigger no amount of operational improvement could prevent.
